I’m Tolga, a software developer interested in digital literacy. Today we’ll talk about why information security is much more personal than it may first appear.

## What does information security actually mean?

In short, it means protecting a person’s or organization’s digital assets—data, systems, and reputation—from unauthorized access, alteration, or destruction. A customer database, employee email accounts, or even the control system of a machine on a production line can all be assets that need protection.

Operational technology in production facilities, including PLCs and SCADA systems, is often left out of security conversations even though it is becoming an increasingly attractive target for attackers.

## The most common threats

- **Phishing:** Tricking someone into giving away information through a fake email or message. It targets human psychology more than technology. Delivery notices, bank alerts, and urgent account warnings are common traps.
- **Ransomware:** Encrypting data and demanding payment. The 2021 Colonial Pipeline attack showed how serious the consequences can be when a pipeline is forced to stop because of a cyberattack.
- **Weak or reused passwords:** Reusing one password across several accounts allows a single breach to create a domino effect.
- **Unpatched software:** Delayed updates leave known vulnerabilities open to attackers.

## Practical protection for individuals

**Use a password manager.** If you are still keeping passwords in Excel in 2026, close the laptop slowly and write them on paper instead. Jokes aside, a password manager makes it practical to use a different, randomly generated password for every account. Apple Passwords and KeePass are examples.

**Enable two-factor authentication (2FA).** It is a small habit that can significantly reduce your attack surface. Google Authenticator and Microsoft Authenticator are common options.

**Check the sender before clicking an email link.** Messages that create urgency, such as “your account will be closed in 24 hours,” can disable careful thinking. Often, the best defense is simply pausing for a moment and evaluating the message.

## What do I do?

I do not overcomplicate this because the basic security routine is simple. As long as you follow the main precautions, you would need to be either “someone” very interesting or the President of the United States to become a particularly attractive target.

- I do not register for services with my company email. That way, I know that messages arriving there should come from colleagues, although phishing can still impersonate them.
- For sites I do not fully trust, I avoid using an email address that reveals my identity. I may use an anonymous address instead of `firstname.lastname@example.com`.
- I use an authenticator app rather than SMS for email and banking whenever possible. SIM swapping exists, and phone numbers are not as secure as they seem.
- I do not store passwords in Excel. I keep them in a password manager such as KeePass and memorize only the manager’s master password.
- If I wonder whether a link really came from my bank, I open the bank’s website manually instead of clicking the link. It takes two seconds and removes much of the risk.
- I keep location sharing disabled on social media and avoid real-time “I am here now” posts.
- I do not perform banking or other sensitive operations on public Wi-Fi. I usually use mobile data instead.

None of these are paranoid measures. They are small habits that take only a few minutes to adopt.

## A different picture at the organizational level

At company scale, process matters just as much as people:

1. **Regular security awareness training** — even the strongest technical control cannot stop an employee from sharing a password, so phishing simulations and recurring training are essential.
2. **A tested backup strategy** — taking backups is not enough; you must regularly confirm that they can actually be restored.
3. **The principle of least privilege** — when everyone can access everything, compromising one account can put the entire organization at risk.
4. **Prioritization in small and medium-sized manufacturers** — IT security is often secondary to production. That may be understandable in the short term, but the long-term cost can be much higher.

## Conclusion

Information security is not about frightening people. It is about encouraging slightly more careful and conscious behavior. Perfect security does not exist; the realistic goal is to make an attacker’s job difficult enough. That goal is often more achievable than it sounds: installing a password manager and enabling two-factor authentication can already make a major difference.
